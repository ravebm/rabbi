<div align="center">

# הַאֲזִינוּ

*ha'azinu* (hah-ah-ZEE-noo) — give ear

</div>

Say it once. *Ha'azinu.* Four syllables, and in the middle of them is *ozen*, the Hebrew word for ear.

It's the first word of the last song Moses ever sang. He is a hundred and twenty years old. He will not cross the river. And he opens his mouth and speaks, not to Israel, but to the sky:

> **"Give ear, O heavens, and I will speak; and let the earth hear the words of my mouth."** — Deuteronomy 32:1 (my translation)
> ***Ha'azinu* hashamayim va'adaberah, v'tishma ha'aretz imrei fi.*

Two verbs for listening in one line. *Ha'azinu*, from the ear: lean in. *Tishma*, from *shema*, Tuesday's word: hear, and do something about it.

Moses asks heaven and earth to listen before he asks the people to. And the rabbis wanted to know why.

Rashi, reading an older teaching, gives the plain answer. Moses is about to die. He needs witnesses who will still be here when he isn't, who will outlast every person in front of him. So he calls the two things that will: the sky and the ground. If Israel keeps faith, let heaven and earth be the ones who reward them. If not, let heaven and earth be the ones who saw.

That is a strange thing to want at the end of a life. Not comfort. Witnesses.

Here is what I hear in it. Moses has spent forty years being listened to badly. Argued with, grumbled at, once nearly stoned. And in his last song he does not ask for a better audience. He asks for a longer one. He wants his words to land somewhere that will still be there in a thousand years. He isn't speaking only to the people in front of him. He is speaking past them. To us.

That's what this Shabbat is. On the Jewish calendar it's called *Shabbat Shuvah*, the Sabbath of Return, the one that falls between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. And the reading from the prophets this morning is the verse this whole week was built on: *"Return, Israel, to the LORD your God."* Monday's word, read aloud today in every congregation on earth.

*The sky was asked to listen before you were, and it's still listening.*

**Today's question:** What did you say this week that you'd want something lasting to have heard?

Now the secret inside the word. It's about where a person is standing.

The rabbis noticed that Moses is not the only prophet who calls heaven and earth to listen. Isaiah does it too, in the first line of his book: *"Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth"* (Isaiah 1:2). Same two verbs. Reversed. Moses says *give ear* to the heavens and *hear* to the earth; Isaiah says *hear* to the heavens and *give ear* to the earth.

The Midrash, in *Sifrei Devarim*, explains the swap. *Give ear* is what you say to someone close: lean in, I'm right here. *Hear* is what you say across a distance. Moses was close to heaven, so he spoke to it intimately and to the earth from far away. Isaiah was a man on the ground, so he did the reverse. The verb tells you where the speaker is standing.

Which means the verse is asking you a question you didn't know it was asking. Not *are you listening*, but *from where?*

You know the one. The person you've been talking to from a distance all week. "Hear me" is what you've been saying, from across the room, from the other end of the argument, when what it needed was the near verb. Lean in. I'm right here.

So before Shabbat ends tonight, say one thing to one person from close instead of from far. Give ear first. Then speak.

*Listening has two verbs in Hebrew. Use the near one.*
