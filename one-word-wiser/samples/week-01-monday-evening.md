This morning: *teshuvah.* Return. Not a feeling — a road.

Tonight, the rabbis.

---

There's a line in the Talmud that has stopped people for fifteen hundred years. It's in tractate Berakhot, and it's spoken by a sage named Rabbi Abbahu:

*"In the place where those who have returned stand, even the perfectly righteous cannot stand."*

Read it again. The person who never left is not standing in the highest place. The person who left and came back is.

That sounds backwards. The rabbis knew it sounded backwards. And they let it stand anyway, because they'd noticed something true: a person who has walked away and turned around knows something about the road that a person who never left cannot know. They know it from both directions.

The Midrash puts a picture on it. It reads a verse from the Song of Songs — *"Open to me, my sister"* — as God speaking, and God says:

*"Open for me an opening the size of the eye of a needle, and I will open for you an opening wide enough for wagons to pass through."*

That's the whole mechanics of *teshuvah* in one sentence. The turn you make can be tiny. A needle's eye. The response is not proportional. It's wagons.

---

**Where Jesus said it.**

Matthew tells us the first thing Jesus said when he began to preach. Not a parable, not a healing. One sentence:

> **"Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has come near."** — Matthew 4:17

Your English Bible says *repent.* The Greek says *metanoeite* — change your mind. But Jesus wasn't speaking Greek to Galilean fishermen. He was speaking to people whose Bible was Hebrew, and the word ringing in their ears was *shuvu.* Return. Come home.

*Come home — the kingdom is near.* That's a very different first sermon than *feel sorry.*

And then, in Luke 15, he tells the *teshuvah* story. A son leaves. Squanders everything. And the text says — this is Luke's phrase — *he came to himself.* Then: *he arose and went to his father.* Two turns. First back to who he is. Then back to where he belongs. That's exactly how the rabbis describe it.

And the father? *While he was still a long way off*, the father saw him and ran. The son opened a needle's eye. The father opened wagons.

---

**The turn.**

So here's the question I'd ask you tonight. Not *what do you regret* — that's the repentance question, and it will keep you up. The *teshuvah* question is smaller and harder:

*Which way are you facing?*

There's probably one thing. One relationship, one habit, one silence. You know the one. You've been walking away from it for a while, and the distance has started to feel like the truth about you.

Maimonides, who wrote the definitive guide to *teshuvah* eight hundred years ago, says the return has four steps. You don't need four tonight. You need one. And it's shorter than you think.

`[PAYWALL]`

**One wiser way.**

Maimonides' four steps are: stop, regret, resolve, and *say it out loud.* Most of us try to do them in that order and stall at the first one. The rabbis' secret is that the last step is the easiest place to start.

So, tonight, before you sleep, one sentence. Out loud. Alone is fine.

*"I've been facing away from ______. I'm turning around."*

Fill in the blank with a name or a thing. Don't add a plan. Don't add an apology yet. Don't explain to yourself why it happened. Just the sentence. The needle's eye is not a feeling you have to generate — it's a direction you say.

Then let it go. You've made the turn. Tomorrow you take a step, and the step is the wagon's job, not yours.

That's *teshuvah.* Not the whole road. The turn.

*Laila tov* — good night.

— Rabbi Evan
