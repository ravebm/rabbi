## Four interpretations

All four actual commentaries were retrieved from Sefaria and read this turn. The page’s English is a concise paraphrase, not a quotation or a claimed published translation.

1. **Rashi, Psalm 130:4:1–2.** Read in Hebrew and the available English. Rashi says that God has not delegated divine forgiveness to an agent, referring to Exodus 23:21. The sheet’s movement toward God is a concise rendering of that emphasis. Do not generalize this to mean that human beings cannot forgive each other.
   https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Psalms.130.4?lang=bi
2. **Ibn Ezra, Psalm 130:4:1.** Read in Hebrew; this endpoint supplied no English. His interpretation says that when God forgives the speaker, other sinners will hear, return, and leave their sin. The page selects that outward effect: one person’s forgiveness can encourage another’s return.
   https://www.sefaria.org/Ibn_Ezra_on_Psalms.130.4?lang=bi
3. **Meiri, Psalm 130:4:1.** Read in Hebrew; this endpoint supplied no English. His first interpretation says that without the promise of forgiveness a sinner might despair, conclude repentance is ineffective, and follow their desires. The sheet selects hope as a reason to keep trying. He also reports the divine-authority reading; the page does not imply that its selected emphasis is his whole commentary. Ibn Ezra and Meiri share a concern with return: the page highlights the social effect in Ibn Ezra and the individual’s hope in Meiri, without inventing a disagreement.
   https://www.sefaria.org/Meiri_on_Psalms.130.4?lang=bi
4. **Malbim, Psalm 130:4:1.** Read in Hebrew; this endpoint supplied no English. The sheet selects his concluding contrast: human power may secure fear through punishment, whereas divine greatness can be revealed by forgiveness and evoke reverence. His earlier theological account of divine forgiveness is not presented as a dictionary definition or used to claim that human forgiveness erases a past act.
   https://www.sefaria.org/Malbim_on_Psalms.130.4?lang=bi
