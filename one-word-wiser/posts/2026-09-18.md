# חַיִּים

*chayim* (chah-YEEM) — life

</div>

Say it once. *Chayim.* You've said it before, at a wedding, with a glass in your hand: *l'chayim*, to life.

Here's the thing nobody mentioned at the wedding. The word is plural.

*Chayim* has the same ending as *mayim*, water, and *shamayim*, sky. Hebrew puts a few things in the plural that English keeps singular, and they're the things you can't count. You can't have one water. You can't have one sky. And in this language you can't have one life. Life comes in the plural, or it doesn't come.

> **"I have set before you life and death, blessings and curses. Now choose life, so that you and your children may live."** — Deuteronomy 30:19 (NIV)
> *...u'vacharta ba**chayim**, l'ma'an tichyeh atah v'zar'echa.*

The word under "life" is *chayim*. Plural. And the "you" who chooses is singular. One person, choosing something that can't be held alone; and the verse doesn't stop at you. *You and your children.*

So "choose life" doesn't mean choose your life. The Hebrew won't let it. It means choose the kind of thing that spills: the meal that feeds more than you, the word that gives someone else room, the decision made with your children in the sentence.

The English says life like it's a possession. The Hebrew says it like it's weather.

*Life, in Hebrew, is plural. You can't choose it just for yourself.*

*The secret inside chayim:* what the daily prayer of these ten days asks God to write, in what book, and why you can't ask it for yourself alone.

`[PAYWALL]`

For ten days a year, from Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur, one line is added to the prayer said three times a day in every synagogue. It's short. *Zochreinu l'chayim, Melech chafetz bachayim, v'kotveinu b'sefer hachayim, l'ma'ancha Elohim chayim.* "Remember us for life, King who desires life, and write us in the book of life, for your sake, living God."

Read the Hebrew again. *Chayim* four times in one breath, and every one of them plural. Life, life, life, life; not one of them singular. The book God is asked to write in is not the book of my life. It's *sefer hachayim*, the book of lives.

It is easy to say that line for years without hearing the grammar. You cannot ask to be written into the book of lives alone. The petition has no singular form. You go in with everyone, or the sentence doesn't parse.

That's the secret of these ten days, hidden in a plural ending. The season feels like a private audit: my year, my wrongs, my name in the book. The prayer refuses. It's *us*, and it's *lives*, and the King who is asked to do the writing is described as one who *wants* them.

That's what God asks in these days. Not a better audit. One more name than your own.

You know the one. The prayer you've been praying for yourself, in the singular, for a long time. It may be waiting for its plural.

So tonight, before Shabbat, put someone else's name in it. Out loud. *Write us.*

*The book of life is a book of lives. Put someone else's name in it tonight.*

Tomorrow: *ha'azinu*, give ear.

— Rabbi Evan
