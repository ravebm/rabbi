# סְלִיחָה

*selichah* (seh-lee-CHAH) — forgiveness

</div>

Say it with me: *selichah*, forgiveness. Let the last syllable take its time.

Think of something you have been explaining away: you were tired, or they misunderstood. There may be truth in every explanation, and still something underneath them waits to be admitted.

*Selichah*, forgiveness, grows from the three-letter root [*salach*](https://biblehub.com/bdb/5545.htm), to pardon: s-l-ch. In biblical Hebrew, this verb names God's forgiveness. The picture is someone standing before God, with nothing left to hide behind, asking to begin again.

That does not mean people cannot forgive. It means this particular biblical word directs our attention to the One we cannot fool.

Listen to where the psalm puts it:

> **“For forgiveness is with You, so that You may be feared.”** — [Psalm 130:4](https://www.chabad.org/library/bible_cdo/aid/16351/jewish/Chapter-130.htm) *(my translation)*
> *Ki imcha* ***ha-selichah*** *lema'an tivare.*

The word here is *ha-selichah*, the forgiveness; the small prefix means “the.” But it is the ending that catches me: “so that You may be feared.” We expect punishment to produce fear; this verse gives forgiveness that place.

I hear reverence in that fear: the seriousness of standing before someone who knows what you did and has not given up on you. *Selichah*, forgiveness, leaves you able to tell the truth. There is someone to tell it to, and a relationship that can hold the telling.

Perhaps you know how quickly shame can turn into another excuse. You defend the small wrong because admitting it feels like admitting you are beyond repair. *Selichah*, forgiveness, lets you name what happened without making it the last thing that can ever happen between you and God.

*Forgiveness gives us somewhere to tell the truth.*

**Today's question:** What could you admit today if you believed that being honest would bring you closer to God?

Tonight: what does receiving another person's apology ask of us when the hurt was real?

— Rabbi Evan
