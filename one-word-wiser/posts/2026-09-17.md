# ‎חֶסֶד

*chesed* (CHEH-sed) — lovingkindness

</div>

About three years ago, I was speaking at a church when someone asked a wonderful, challenging question: “What is the Hebrew word for grace?”

It was harder to answer than you might think. Grace carries a whole world of meaning in Christian faith.

My first answer was *chen*, **חֵן**, meaning grace or favor. It is the word Genesis uses when Noah finds favor in God’s eyes. ([Genesis 6:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6%3A8&version=NIV))

But there’s a different kind of grace in Hebrew. It’s called *chesed*.

Think of someone who helped you through a hard time. Someone who noticed what you needed before you asked.

## Inside the Word

The root letters are **ח · ס · ד**, chet, samekh, dalet. *Chesed* can mean kindness, loyalty, or steadfast love.

> **“Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good. His love endures forever.”** — [Psalm 136:1 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+136%3A1&version=NIV)
>
> *Hodu l’Adonai ki tov, ki l’olam **chasdo**.*

“His love” is *chasdo*, **חַסְדּוֹ**: *chesed* with an ending meaning “his.” Here, it is God’s love.

The psalm repeats that refrain twenty-six times. It moves from God making the heavens to God remembering people brought low and feeding living creatures. ([Psalm 136](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+136&version=NIV))

**The God who made the heavens also notices who is hungry.**

That is an extraordinary way to understand God’s power. It takes the form of care.

For us, that care begins with noticing. The unanswered message. The person who has stopped asking for help.

But what we do next matters. Even kindness can leave someone feeling small.

**How do we help someone without making it harder to need us?**

*The Talmud finds an answer in two Hebrew letters: one reaching forward, the other facing away.*

[PAYWALL]

## The Kindness You Never Forget

In the Talmud, children studying the alphabet turn two neighboring letters into a lesson in giving. ([Shabbat 104a](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.104a?lang=en))

<h1 dir="rtl" style="text-align: center">ג ד</h1>

*Read from right to left: gimel, then dalet.*

They hear in the letters’ names *gemol dalim*: give to the poor.

The leg of **ג**, *gimel*, reaches toward **ד**, *dalet*. In their reading, the giver should seek out the person who needs help.

But *dalet* faces away. The children read this as a call to give discreetly, protecting the recipient from embarrassment.

The two gestures belong together. Notice the need. Respect the person’s privacy.

## Make It Easier to Receive

Noticing someone’s need does not mean deciding for them.

“Would it help if I brought dinner on Tuesday?” is an offer they can accept or decline. They need not explain everything that has happened to deserve a meal.

If they accept, bring it. Keep their circumstances private. Let them decide how much to share.

This is what I hear in *chesed*: care that pays attention to how it feels to receive it.

The people whose kindness we remember often understood this. They noticed us at a vulnerable moment and allowed us to remain ourselves.

*They helped us feel seen without making us feel small.*

— Rabbi Evan
