# חֶסֶד

*chesed* (CHEH-sed) — loyal love

</div>

Say it once. *Chesed.* The first sound comes from the back of the throat, like the *ch* in Bach, and the stress is up front.

## A Word English Can't Finish

English has been trying to translate this word for centuries and hasn't finished. The old translators had to invent a word, "lovingkindness," to get close. The NIV says "love." Others say "mercy," "steadfast love," "faithful love." Each one is a piece.

## One Key Verse

> **"Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good. His love endures forever."** — Psalm 136:1 (NIV)
> *Hodu l'Adonai ki tov, ki l'olam **chasdo**.*

The word under "love" is *chesed*. The psalm repeats that line twenty-six times, once in every verse.

## Love That Has Already Decided

Here is what the English keeps missing. *Chesed* is not a feeling you have toward someone. It's what you do for someone you're bound to, when nothing forces you to. It's Ruth walking to Bethlehem beside a mother-in-law who has nothing left to give her. It's a love that has already decided.

Mercy is what you show a stranger. Kindness is what you show anyone. *Chesed* is what you show the people you've promised yourself to, on the days you'd rather not.

That is why it can be "forever." A feeling can't promise that. A commitment can.

<p style="text-align: center">— <strong>ח</strong> —</p>

*Other mentions:* Luke 10:37. Asked which man was the neighbor, the lawyer answers "the one who had mercy on him." That is *chesed*'s word. The Samaritan didn't feel something. He kept showing up.

## Let It Change Your Life

*But the Jewish sages asked a simple question about the whole Torah, the five books of Moses. What is the first thing God does for a human being, and what is the last? Both answers, they said, are acts of chesed. What are they?*

`[PAYWALL]`

The question is asked in the Talmud by a sage named Rabbi Simlai. Not the first word of the Torah and the last word. The first thing God does for a person, and the last.

The first comes right after the garden, after the fig leaves: "The LORD God made garments of skin for Adam and his wife and clothed them" (Genesis 3:21). God dresses two people who have just failed him.

The last comes on a mountain in Moab, with no one else there: "he buried him" (Deuteronomy 34:6). God digs Moses's grave, because there is no one else to do it.

Rabbi Simlai's point: the Torah begins with *chesed* and ends with *chesed*. Clothing the naked and burying the dead. Everything else, all the law, all the story, sits between two acts of loyal love that nobody could repay.

<p style="text-align: center">— <strong>ח</strong> —</p>

That's the definition the sages wanted kept. Not love as warmth. Love as the thing you do for someone who cannot possibly pay you back.

So today, one act of *chesed* for someone who can't repay it.

*God dresses the ashamed and buries the dead. Everything else is in between.*

Tomorrow: *chayim*, life.

— Rabbi Evan
