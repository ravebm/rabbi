# חֶסֶד

*chesed* (CHEH-sed) — loyal love

</div>

Say it once. *Chesed.* The first sound from the back of the throat, like the *ch* in Bach, and the stress up front.

English has been trying to translate this word for centuries and hasn't finished. The old translators had to invent a word, "lovingkindness," to get close. The NIV says "love." Others say "mercy," "steadfast love," "faithful love." Each one is a piece.

> **"Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good. His love endures forever."** — Psalm 136:1 (NIV)
> *Hodu l'Adonai ki tov, ki l'olam **chasdo**.*

The word under "love" is *chesed*. The psalm says that line twenty-six times, once per verse, the way you'd say something to a child who isn't sure yet.

Here is what the English keeps missing. *Chesed* is not a feeling you have toward someone. It's what you do for someone you're bound to, when nothing forces you to. It's Ruth walking to Bethlehem beside a mother-in-law who has nothing left to give her. It's a love that has already decided.

Mercy is what you show a stranger. Kindness is what you show anyone. *Chesed* is what you show the people you've promised yourself to, on the days you'd rather not.

Which is why it can be "forever." A feeling can't promise that. A commitment can.

*Other mentions:* Luke 10:37. Asked which man was the neighbor, the lawyer answers "the one who had mercy on him." That is *chesed*'s word. The Samaritan didn't feel something. He kept showing up.

*Chesed isn't how you feel about someone. It's what you've decided to do.*

*The secret inside chesed:* the two acts of chesed the Talmud says the whole Torah is wrapped in, one at each end, and what they ask of you for the person who can't pay you back.

`[PAYWALL]`

In the Talmud, in tractate Sotah, a sage named Rabbi Simlai asks how the Torah begins and how it ends. Not the first word and the last word. The first thing God does for a person, and the last.

The first: after the garden, after the fig leaves, "The LORD God made garments of skin for Adam and his wife and clothed them" (Genesis 3:21). God dresses two people who have just failed him.

The last: on a mountain in Moab, with no one else there, "he buried him" (Deuteronomy 34:6). God digs Moses's grave, because there is no one else to do it.

Rabbi Simlai's point: the Torah begins with *chesed* and ends with *chesed*. Clothing the naked and burying the dead. Everything else, all the law, all the story, is held between two acts of loyal love that nobody could repay.

That's the definition the rabbis wanted kept. Not love as warmth. Love as the thing you do for someone who cannot possibly pay you back: the newly ashamed, the newly dead.

That's what God asks. Not that you feel more. That you show up for the one who can't return it.

You know the one. Someone in your life right now is in one of those two places, ashamed or grieving, and can't return anything to you. That's not the reason to wait. That's the whole reason.

So today, one act of *chesed* for someone who can't repay it. Don't tell them it's a word.

*God dresses the ashamed and buries the dead. Everything else is in between.*

Tomorrow: *chayim*, life.

— Rabbi Evan
