This morning, *selichah*, forgiveness, gave us somewhere to tell the truth before God.

*You can accept an apology without pretending the hurt never happened.*

`[PAYWALL]`

Picture the moment Maimonides describes in his [Laws of Repentance, 2:10](https://www.chabad.org/library/article_cdo/aid/911891/jewish/Teshuvah-Chapter-Two.htm). Someone has wronged you. Now that person asks your forgiveness.

Maimonides turns toward you, the one receiving the apology. He asks you to forgive with “a whole heart and a willing spirit” (my translation). He also tells you to set aside revenge and the holding of a grudge.

Notice where he puts the heart. Saying the right words is only part of it. He is asking what happens after the conversation ends, when you could bring the old injury out again to make the other person small.

The apology sits between two people: one has had to ask, and the other has to decide whether there will ever be an answer. Maimonides asks us to make that answer possible, to let a sincere request reach us instead of keeping the person forever at the moment they did wrong.

You know the one: someone apologized, and you have kept the reply waiting. Consider an ordinary hurt where the apology is sincere and contact is safe; forgiveness can begin there, while trust may take longer. You do not have to offer renewed closeness or put yourself back within reach of harm.

Tonight, write one sentence you could honestly say in response to that apology, without sending it yet.

*You can remember what happened and still let something new happen.*

*Laila tov* — good night. Tomorrow: *chesed*, loyal love.

— Rabbi Evan
