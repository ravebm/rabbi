This morning, *chesed*, loyal love, gave us a word for care that continues after the first impulse has passed.

*Some of the holiest things we do will never be repaid.*

`[PAYWALL]`

In the Talmud, Sotah 14a, Rabbi Simlai places two scenes beside each other. Near the beginning of the Torah, God makes garments for Adam and Eve and clothes them. Near its end, God buries Moses.

Hold those two scenes for a moment. Adam and Eve have eaten the forbidden fruit, and their disobedience has consequences, yet God attends to their need for clothing. At the other end of the Torah, Moses has died and can lead no further, yet he is cared for in burial.

Rabbi Simlai teaches that the Torah begins and ends with acts of kindness. Between those scenes lie all its commandments and arguments. He asks us to see the care at either end.

*Chesed*, loyal love, takes a bodily form in his reading: a garment, a burial. God's care reaches people in their vulnerability. What stays with me is that simple attention to a person's need, even when nothing can be offered in return.

You may be thinking of someone tonight who has needed more help than you expected. You know the one: the calls have become harder to make, and you are tired, too. *Chesed*, loyal love, can begin with one manageable kindness; it need not require a promise beyond what you can give.

Tomorrow morning, offer that person one specific kindness you can carry through, such as bringing a meal on a day you name.

*Your kindness matters even when no thank-you comes back.*

May you receive the care you need, too. *Laila tov* — good night. Tomorrow: *chayim*, life.

— Rabbi Evan
