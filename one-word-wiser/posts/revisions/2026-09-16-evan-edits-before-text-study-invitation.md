<h1 style="text-align: center;">‎סְלִיחָה</h1>

<p style="text-align: center;"><em>selichah</em> (seh-lee-CHAH) — forgiveness</p>

At the end of Genesis, Joseph’s brothers are afraid.

Their father has died. The brother they once sold into slavery now has the power to repay them. Or take revenge.

They wonder whether he has been waiting for this moment.

Joseph answers with a sentence that leaves no doubt about what happened: **“You intended to harm me.”**

But then kindness returns. Then he promises to provide for them and their children. He speaks kindly to them. ([Genesis 50:15–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+50%3A15-21&version=NIV))

He remembers who they are. And he looks to the future. He doesn’t pretend the hurt was not real! That’s not what forgiveness asks. that’s a fundamental misunderstanding.

In the Bible forgiveness has a different purpose.

### Inside the Word

> **“But with God there is forgiveness, so that we can serve You.”** — [Psalm 130:4 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+130%3A4&version=NIV)
>
> *Ki imcha ha**selichah**, l’ma’an tivarei.*

*Selichah* generally means **forgiveness**. Its three root letters are **ס · ל · ח**, samekh, lamed, chet.

In biblical Hebrew, that verb describes God’s forgiveness.

But the Bible has more than one word for forgiving.

When Joseph’s brothers ask him to forgive them, the verb comes from a different root: *nasa*, which can mean **lift**, **carry**, or **forgive**, depending on the sentence. It has a HUMAN element to it.

But English gathers these different Hebrew words under one word: forgiveness. Humans forgive differently than God forgives. It takes a little more from us.

OUR RESPONSIBILITY

Sometimes we hesitate to forgive because we are still waiting for someone to understand what they did.

We need them to remember the promise they broke. The words they said in front of everyone. The time we needed them and they did not come.

Jewish teaching takes that need seriously. There is no INSTANT FORGIVENESS.

Maimonides, the twelfth-century rabbi and legal scholar, says that when we have wronged another person, we must restore what we owe and seek that person’s forgiveness.

Even returning the money does not finish the work. We still have to approach the person we hurt. ([Laws of Repentance 2:9](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Repentance.2.9?lang=bi))

## Begin With What You Can Repair

We all have done things for which we seek forgiveness.

So before asking to be forgiven, name what happened. And don’t explain it away. Consider what you can put right. Then give the other person room to answer.

Joseph’s kindness did not change what his brothers had done. It changed what he would do next.

— Rabbi Evan
