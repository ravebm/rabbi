# ‎חֶסֶד

*chesed* (CHEH-sed) — lovingkindness

</div>

About three years ago I was speaking a church and someone asked a wonderful challenging question:  What is the Hebrew word for grace?”

It was harder to answer than you might think.

Grace carries a whole world of meaning in Christian faith. A single Hebrew word cannot capture every part of it.

My first answer was the word *chen*, **חֵן**, which means goodness or favor.

When Genesis tells  Noah found *favor* in God’s eyes, that is the Hebrew word it uses. ([Genesis 6:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+6%3A8&version=NIV))

But there’s a different kind of grace in Hebrew. It’s called *Chesed*.

Think of someone who helped you through a hard time.

Someone who noticed you. Who saw what you needed  without even asking. That’s grace. And the Hebrew Bible has a powerful word for that.

## Inside the Word

The three root letters of *chesed* are **ח · ס · ד**, chet, samekh, dalet. The word can express kindness, loyalty, or steadfast love, depending on the passage.

> **“Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good. His love endures forever.”** — [Psalm 136:1 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+136%3A1&version=NIV)
>
> *Hodu l’Adonai ki tov, ki l’olam **chasdo**.*

The Hebrew under “his love” is *chasdo*, **חַסְדּוֹ**.

It is *chesed* with an ending that means **“his.”** Here, it is God’s love.

The psalm repeats its refrain twenty-six times, once in every verse: *His love endures forever.*

Look at the power of this verse!

At first, we hear about God making the heavens, the sun, and the stars.

By the end, we hear about God remembering people brought low and giving food to living creatures. ([Psalm 136](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+136&version=NIV))

**The God who made the heavens also notices who is hungry.**

That is an extraordinary way to understand God’s power. It takes the form of care.

*Chesed* gives us a word for that care. Love that notices. Love that takes responsibility for another person.

And it asks something of us.

We may never know exactly what someone else is going through. But we can notice the empty chair, the unanswered message, the person who has stopped asking for help.

**How do we help someone without making it harder to need us?**

*The Talmud preserves a remarkable answer from children studying two Hebrew letters. They found a lesson in how to give, and when to turn away.*

## The Kindness You Never Forget

In the Talmud, sages report that young students have offered remarkable interpretations of the alphabet. Two neighboring letters become a lesson in giving. ([Shabbat 104a](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.104a?lang=en))

<h1 dir="rtl" style="text-align: center">ג ד</h1>

*Read from right to left: gimel, then dalet.*

The children hear in their names *gemol dalim*: give to the poor.

Look at the extended leg of **ג**, *gimel*. In their reading, it reaches toward **ד**, *dalet*. The giver should seek out the person who needs help.

Then they notice that *dalet* faces away. They read this as a call to give discreetly, so that receiving help does not bring embarrassment.

It is an imaginative teaching about the alphabet. And it asks us to pay attention to something easy to overlook: **the experience of the person receiving our kindness.**

We can be so eager to help that we forget to ask what would actually help. We offer advice when someone needs company. We tell others what we have done, sharing a story that was never ours to tell.

## Make It Easier to Receive

Think of one person going through a difficult time. Consider a specific offer: “Would it help if I brought dinner on Tuesday?” Give them room to choose, including room to decline.

If they accept, let the help arrive without requiring the whole story. Keep their circumstances private. Let them decide how much they want to say.

The people whose kindness we remember often understood this. They helped us through a vulnerable moment and allowed us to remain ourselves.

When I think about grace, I think about being able to receive love without having to make a case for myself.

*We can make it a little easier for someone else to receive that kind of love today.*

— Rabbi Evan
