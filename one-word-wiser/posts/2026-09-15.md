# שְׁמַע

*shema* (sheh-MAH) — listen

</div>

**In English:** hear.
**In Hebrew:** *shema* — listen; take heed.

Someone you love says, “You’re not listening.”

You could repeat every word they just said. You were already preparing your answer.

That is the difficulty today’s Hebrew word asks us to sit with.

## One Key Verse

> **“Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one.”** — [Deuteronomy 6:4 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+6%3A4&version=NIV)<br>
> ***Shema** Yisrael, Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai echad.*

*Shema* is a command: listen. It opens the prayer recited morning and evening in Jewish tradition.

The Hebrew verb behind it, *shama*, can mean to hear, to listen, or to heed. In [Exodus 19:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+19%3A5&version=NIV), the NIV translates a form of that same verb as “obey,” as God calls Israel to keep the covenant.

Context matters. Sometimes the word simply describes hearing a sound. Here, at the heart of prayer, listening asks something of us.

The next verse commands love of God with the whole heart, soul, and strength. First listen. Then love. I think that order has something to teach us.

*Other mentions:* In [Mark 12:29](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+12%3A29&version=NIV), Jesus begins his answer about the most important commandment with this same verse.

## Two Larger Letters

In the traditional Hebrew text, two letters stand larger than their neighbors: the final *ayin* in *shema* and the final *dalet* in *echad*, one.

Put them together and they spell *ed*, witness.

The Baal HaTurim, a medieval Jewish commentator, [draws attention to those letters](https://www.sefaria.org/Kitzur_Ba%27al_HaTurim_on_Deuteronomy.6.4). He connects them with Israel’s calling to bear witness to God.

For me, that makes the prayer a question about the life that follows it. When I finish speaking about God, how will I speak to the next person?

<p style="text-align: center">— <strong>ש</strong> —</p>

## Before You Answer

Often that person is someone we love. They are trying to explain why something hurt. We remember the conversation differently. We have reasons. We want our turn.

It takes patience to let them finish. Especially when we feel misunderstood ourselves.

But there is something we can do before explaining our intentions: find out what they experienced. Ask what hurt. Listen to the answer long enough that we could describe it fairly.

We may still disagree. We may need to say something difficult. Understanding someone gives us a better place to begin.

I think this is one reason a prayer can begin with a word we need to hear twice a day. Listening takes practice, even with the people we know best.

Today, when you notice yourself composing an answer while someone is speaking, leave it unfinished for a moment.

*Let them finish first.*

— Rabbi Evan
