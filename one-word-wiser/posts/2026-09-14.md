Say it once, out loud. *Teshuvah.* The stress lands on the last syllable, like a step landing on a doorstep.

> In English: repent.
>
> In Hebrew: teshuvah — come home.

The English translation is usually “repentance.” It’s a strange one because the Hebrew has nothing do it with it.

*Teshuvah* grows from a three-letter root: *shuv.* To turn. To turn around. To come back.

It’s one of the most common verbs in the whole Hebrew Bible — it shows up over a thousand times — and almost every time it means something physical.

A man *turns* back on the road.

A river *returns* to its course.

A spouses *comes* home.

<p style="text-align: center">— <strong>ת</strong> —</p>

Repentance, in English, is a feeling.

It comes from a Latin word for penalty.

It happens inside you.

*Teshuvah* is not a feeling. It’s a direction.

You were walking one way. You turn around. You walk back.

## One Key Verse

The prophet Hosea gives it to Israel at the very end of his book, after fourteen chapters of heartbreak:

> **“Return, Israel, to the LORD your God.”** — Hosea 14:1 (NIV; 14:2 in the Hebrew)<br>
> ***Shuvah** Yisrael ad Adonai Elohecha.*

The Hebrew is a command: **Return!**

Not *be ashamed, Israel.* Just: *turn around. Come home. Now.*

## You Already Belong

Notice what the verse doesn’t say. It doesn’t say fix yourself first. It doesn’t say become worthy.

It says *return*.

The relationship isn’t waiting on the other side of the turn. It’s the entire reason you can turn. God makes it possible.

That’s the whole difference.

Repentance asks: *how bad do I feel?*

*Teshuvah* asks: *which way should I face now?*

## Learning to Return

But the Jewish sages said one fascinating thing about teshuvah.

They said a person who has done *teshuvah* is more righteous than a person who never had to make *teshuvah*.

#### So a person who has sinned is more righteous than someone who has not?

The Talmud, the great collection of rabbinic debate, gives an answer.

A sage says it almost in passing:

*“In the place where those who have returned stand, even the perfectly righteous cannot stand.”*

Read it again. The person who never left is not in the highest place. The person who left and came back is.

The rabbis noticed a basic human truth: someone who has sinned and returned has gone through more than they can put into words.

They know how hard the road is. They know what the turning costs. They know the road from both directions.

<p style="text-align: center">— <strong>ת</strong> —</p>

And the sages add another teaching, one closely paralleling the parable of the prodigal son.

God says: *Open for me an opening the size of the eye of a needle, and I will open for you an opening wide enough for wagons.*

I hear it this way: *Start to return home, and I will come and bring you back with open arms.*
