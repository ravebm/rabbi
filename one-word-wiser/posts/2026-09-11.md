Tonight begins the Jewish New Year, Rosh Hashanah. May your year be filled with blessings.

A new year seemed like the right time for a new beginning here, too.

This newsletter has gone through a few iterations over the years. But the heart of it has never changed. I share Jewish wisdom for people all faiths.

And over the years, one question has reached me more than any other.

Not about God. Not about my books.

But, funny enough, about Hebrew! About words. This happened especially after my last book,*[Reading the Old Testament Through Jewish Eyes.](https://www.amazon.com/Reading-Testament-Through-Jewish-Eyes-ebook/dp/B08LQXGS9V/?_encoding=UTF8&pd_rd_w=W2UI4&content-id=amzn1.sym.171c1bd3-e559-4de5-8b1c-2e86c02e2e84&pf_rd_p=171c1bd3-e559-4de5-8b1c-2e86c02e2e84&pf_rd_r=131-8781544-5420508&pd_rd_wg=i2MSM&pd_rd_r=3a54d76c-d0a0-4ced-9fb8-35c4fe1ab8ad)*

**A word before the word.**

See, Hebrew is not like other languages. Every Hebrew letter has a shape and a story.

*Bet* is shaped like a house, *Ayin* looks like an eye, *Shin* looks like a flame. Hebrew began as a spoken language, so its words carry layers of nuance we can explore.

And words that seem unrelated in English turn out to be family in Hebrew. Hope and Rope; Repent and Return; Year and Change.

When you learn one Hebrew word, you don’t learn a definition. You learn how God’s language sees the thing.

And that matters, because in the Torah, God creates with words. God says, “Let there be light,” and there is light.

The Jewish taught that the whole world was spoken into being with ten utterances. Nothing was hammered or carved. It was said into being. Words have power in the Biblical tradition.

Some scholars even trace the magician’s phrase “abracadabra,” which we all know, to the Hebrew phrase meaning I create as I speak.

Speaking is a form of human creativity. And Hebrew is the language of God’s creation itself.

I’ll also explore how Jewish teachers have read these words, and what their readings can bring to everyday life.

That’s all. One word, every morning.

My hope is that you’ll feel closer to God’s word than you ever have. And a little closer to yourself.

Happy New Year,<br>

Every year, you promise to change. To change something important. And every year, the same things come back.

Hebrew has a word for exactly that.

*Shanah.* The word for year.

**How can one word mean both** ***change*** **and** ***repeat*** **at the same time?** And what does that say about life?

Its three letters — *shin, nun, hey* — give us *mishnah*, repetition, and *sheni*, second. The same three letters give us *shinui*, change. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks called it the paradox of time, held in a single word: “to change and to repeat.”

Seasons repeat. But we change.

Situations repeat. And we can change how we meet them.

The conversation that always turns into an argument comes around again. This time, we listen a little longer.

Someone we love makes the same mistake. We remember how often we’ve needed another chance ourselves.

Sometimes the change is almost invisible. A softer answer. Or the courage to say the thing we’ve been holding back.

We usually wait for life to change before we live differently. A new year asks us to begin the other way around.
