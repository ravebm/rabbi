ONE WORD WISER

Listen With Your Heart

Rabbi Evan Moffic

‎שְׁמַע

shema (sheh-MAH) — listen

We don’t listen with our ears. We listen with our heart.

You can hear every word someone says and miss what they are trying to tell you. A child tells you about her day. You hear the events. Do you hear the loneliness?

We say seeing is believing. But seeing someone’s face does not tell us what it feels like to be that person. For that, we have to listen.

Inside the Word

“Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one.” — Deuteronomy 6:4 (NIV)

Shema Yisrael, Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai echad.

Shema is a command: listen. Its three root letters are ש · מ · ע, shin, mem, ayin. Follow this root through the Bible, and you find more than one kind of hearing.

Hear a sound. In Genesis 3:8, Adam and Eve hear God in the garden and hide. The verb comes from this same root. They hear, but they do not draw closer.

Listen with understanding. When Solomon asks for wisdom, he asks for a lev shomea, לֵב שֹׁמֵעַ, a listening heart. The NIV renders it “a discerning heart” (1 Kings 3:9). Shomea means “listening,” another form of our verb. Solomon needs to judge fairly. His heart must do more than feel; it must understand.

Respond to what you hear. In Exodus 19:5, God calls Israel to keep the covenant. Here the NIV translates the Hebrew phrase “obey me fully.” The verb is from the same root again. In this setting, listening calls for action.

One root, different contexts. English uses hear, discern, and obey to bring out what each passage means. The Hebrew lets us notice a connection: listening can ask something of us.

Hearken

The King James Bible gives us an old English word worth recovering: hearken. In Deuteronomy 11:13, it uses “hearken diligently” for a phrase built from this same Hebrew root. To hearken is to listen attentively, to heed.

Long before that English translation, the early rabbis understood the Shema as an act of yielding to God. The Mishnah calls it accepting “the yoke of the kingdom of Heaven.” First we accept God’s authority; then we take on the commandments.

I think of this as a moment of surrender: I am ready to be guided, even when what I hear is not what I expected.

Jewish tradition gives that inward movement a physical form. A small gesture helps us set aside what competes for our attention and listen with the heart.

The story behind that gesture, and a way to practice it, follow below.

Close Your Eyes to Listen

There is a Jewish practice that makes this visible: covering our eyes when we say the first verse of the Shema.

The Talmud tells a small story about Rabbi Judah HaNasi, a great teacher. Rav wondered when he recited the Shema; he had not seen him do it.

Rabbi Hiyya explained what Rav had missed. During his lesson, Rabbi Judah passed his hands over his face. In that moment, he was accepting God’s sovereignty through the Shema.

A gesture so small that a student could miss it. Yet it held a prayer.

A later Jewish law code explains the custom of covering the eyes: it keeps what we see from distracting our attention. We give these few words a moment of their own.

Try it with the verse above. Sit quietly. Cover your eyes gently and say the words slowly, in Hebrew or English. Attend to their meaning. When your mind wanders, return to the words.

You do not have to produce a special feeling. You are making room to be present.

Let this be a moment of surrender. You might silently add, “God, help me receive what You are asking of me.” Then return to the words of the prayer.

Then carry something of that attention into a conversation. Put down the phone. Let the other person finish. Listen for what matters enough that they needed to tell you.

You can ask, “What was that like for you?” And leave enough silence for an answer you did not expect.

We cover our eyes for prayer. We open them again to the person before us.

May we bring our heart to both.

— Rabbi Evan
