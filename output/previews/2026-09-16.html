Forgiveness Doesn’t Erase What Happened

You can tell the truth about the hurt and still hope for something different.

Rabbi Evan Moffic · September 16, 2026

סְלִיחָה

selichah (seh-lee-CHAH) — forgiveness

At the end of Genesis, Joseph’s brothers are afraid.

Their father has died. The brother they once sold into slavery now has the power to repay them. They wonder whether he has been waiting for this moment.

Joseph answers with a sentence that leaves no doubt about what happened: “You intended to harm me.”

Then he promises to provide for them and their children. He speaks kindly to them. (Genesis 50:15–21)

He remembers. And he makes room for a future with them.

That matters when forgiveness feels like being asked to say the hurt was never real.

Inside the Word

“But with you there is forgiveness, so that we can, with reverence, serve you.” — Psalm 130:4 (NIV)

Ki imcha haselichah, l’ma’an tivarei.

Selichah means forgiveness. Its three root letters are ס · ל · ח, samekh, lamed, chet.

In this verse, ha-selichah is the noun with the Hebrew word for “the” attached. The related verb, salach, means to forgive or pardon. In biblical Hebrew, that verb describes God’s forgiveness.

But the Bible has more than one word for forgiving.

When Joseph’s brothers ask him to forgive them, the verb comes from a different root: nasa, which can mean lift, carry, or forgive, depending on the sentence. The Bible uses this verb of God’s forgiveness, too. (Exodus 34:7)

English gathers these different Hebrew words under one word: forgiveness. Each passage shows us a little more of what it means.

Joseph names the harm and chooses to care for the people who caused it. In the psalm, forgiveness makes it possible to approach God again. The person praying can still stand in a relationship with God.

— ס —

What Forgiveness Leaves Intact

Sometimes we hesitate to forgive because we are still waiting for someone to understand what they did.

We need them to remember the promise they broke. The words they said in front of everyone. The time we needed them and they did not come.

Jewish teaching takes that need seriously.

Maimonides, the twelfth-century rabbi and legal scholar, says that when we have wronged another person, we must restore what we owe and seek that person’s forgiveness. Even returning the money does not finish the work. We still have to approach the person we hurt. (Laws of Repentance 2:9)

The damage matters enough to repair. The person matters enough to ask.

I hear an invitation in this: we can hope to be forgiven while taking full responsibility for what needs to change.

Begin With What You Can Repair

Think of one small hurt for which you owe an apology.

Before asking to be forgiven, name what happened without explaining it away. Consider what you can put right. Then give the other person room to answer.

If you are the one who was hurt, you may need time. Trust may need to be rebuilt. You can keep the boundaries that protect you.

Joseph’s kindness did not change what his brothers had done. It changed what he would do next.
